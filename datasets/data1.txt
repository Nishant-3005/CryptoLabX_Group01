The history of cryptography dates back thousands of years. Ancient civilizations used simple
substitution ciphers to protect sensitive messages. The Caesar cipher, one of the earliest known
encryption techniques, was used by Julius Caesar to communicate with his generals. Each letter
in the plaintext was shifted by a fixed number of positions in the alphabet.

For example, with a shift of three, the letter A becomes D, B becomes E, and so on. The
recipient, knowing the shift value, could reverse the process to read the original message.
While revolutionary for its time, this cipher is trivially broken today using frequency analysis
or simply by trying all twenty-six possible shifts.

Cryptography evolved significantly during the Renaissance with the invention of polyalphabetic
ciphers such as the Vigenere cipher. These schemes used a keyword to apply different shifts to
different letters, making frequency analysis far more difficult. For centuries the Vigenere
cipher was considered unbreakable, earning it the nickname le chiffre indechiffrable.

The twentieth century brought mechanical encryption machines like the Enigma, used by Nazi
Germany during World War II. Allied codebreakers at Bletchley Park, including Alan Turing,
broke the Enigma cipher, a feat that is widely believed to have shortened the war by several
years. Modern cryptography now relies on complex mathematical problems that even the most
powerful computers cannot solve in a reasonable amount of time.
